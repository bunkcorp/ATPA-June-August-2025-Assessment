### 1.1.3 Module Introduction

This module introduces several examples of negative outcomes arising from
modern technology, including big data, predictive models, and artificial
intelligence. The main purpose of such examples is to illustrate that there
could be negative unintended consequences due to reasons such as ill-will
of agents, misuse, abuse, negligence of users, ignorance, cognitive
fallacies, and underlying biases, both conscious and unconscious, of
people.
Especially in this information age, regulations have been evolving to
prevent negative impact from these powerful new technologies. However,
regulations are often slow to adapt to the fast-paced, evolving world.
Also, regulations act as a boundary or limit rather than as guidance for
day-to-day decision making. As such, ethical frameworks need to be
considered and applied to provide guidance for the actuarial community.
This module is intended to help you learn to use technology in a
responsible way to provide value to stakeholders and the public. Two
examples provide motivation for this material.

### 1.1.5 Example: Non-Transparent or Unexplainable Outcomes

To understand whether results are fair, one should be able to test the
fairness using certain attributes. Non-transparent models or unexplainable
outcomes, even if the predictions are accurate, can be an issue. The
following example illustrates why this can be a problem.
In the criminal justice system, recidivism risk assessments are commonly
used to predict a defendant’s likelihood of becoming a repeat offender.
These tools are often based on advanced statistical models and trained on a
variety of data points, including age, employment history, and prior
criminal records. The scores derived from these tools are used across
different stages of the criminal justice process; this varies by state.
They influence decisions that affect defendants’ freedom, including setting
bail, releasing on parole, and even in sentencing.
There are concerns about the potential for bias and discrimination in these
models. One challenge is the transparency of predictions. As pointed out in
a Harvard Data Science Review article titled "The Age of Secrecy and
Unfairness in Recidivism Prediction" about one such model, COMPAS, the
authors noted the model’s complexity (it is based on up to 137 variables,
many of which are collected from questionnaires and hence may be subject to
data entry and other types of errors) and lack of transparency (the
underlying formulas are proprietary). These factors pose clear challenges
to ensuring fairness (Rudin, Wang, & Coker, 2020). It is especially
important that models being used to make important decisions regarding
people are as transparent as possible to protect the rights of individuals.
This example is discussed in more detail in a later section.
It is more difficult to diagnose and correct errors in a complicated model.
In addition, the authors noted that faulty assumptions about a “black box”
model lead to faulty conclusions. Transparency helps avoid these
situations. Transparency provides two main benefits with respect to
fairness. First, “it is easier to debate the fairness of a transparent
model than a proprietary model.” Second, transparency provides an
opportunity for remediation. With a life-changing decision, such as those
impacted by recidivism risk assessments, a clear and verifiable explanation
offers “procedural fairness.”

### 1.1.7 Introduction

Due to the increasing use of big data and predictive models, there are more
potential concerns and risks that need to be understood and considered. The
technology associated with the unprecedented amount of data creates new
challenges for us. Regulations and ethical frameworks have been emerging to
respond to such challenges. The increased use of automation and predictive
modeling results in less human review and intervention, creating a new
class of ethical risks that were not previously encountered by the
actuarial profession.* This section provides an introduction to ethical
values and ethical principles.
As an example, insurance companies may consider collecting prescription
history, credit history, geographic, biometric, and genetic data. To what
extent can organizations legally and ethically collect this data? Is it
acceptable to collect and use this data for pricing and underwriting an
insurance contract even if a customer agrees? What is the right thing to
do? This section provides a foundation and framework for participants to
address these types of questions. An ethical framework guides why some
decisions are more desirable than others; it also helps us to decide which
data and models are permissible in a given situation. This is important, as
some of these decisions are context-dependent.
* Because this material is intended as education for actuaries, we will
often speak of the “actuarial profession.” However, the concepts in this
module apply throughout the insurance industry and broader financial
services.
Introduction


### 1.1.8 Ethics

Generally, ethics refers to standards of behavior defining how individuals,
a community, or society should act morally and responsibly in various
situations. A succinct definition describes ethics as “the discipline
dealing with what is good and bad and with moral duty and obligation”
(Merriam Webster, 2020). A critical component of ethics is the obligation
or responsibility to a larger group of people than an individual or
organization and their direct relationships. This module focuses on the
application of ethics in a professional setting.

### 1.1.10 Ethical Principles

Regardless of who we are, we order our lives by certain sets of principles.
The principles may require a certain degree of interpretation, depending on
the situation. Additionally, certain principles may conflict, and
practitioners may need to balance the trade-offs. For example, a more
robust and accurate model may be a black-box model, which may be a
challenge to explain. A practitioner will need to decide which principles
are a priority–a model that is more robust or one that is more transparent.
Ethical Principles


### 1.1.11 Ethical Principles and Framework

This module uses three broad groups to discuss ethical principles:
1. Fairness,
2. Safety,
3. Transparency and Accountability.
Some of the principles may be connected and intertwined. For example,
transparency and accountability are interrelated. If a model is not
transparent and the cause of certain issues (such as biases) cannot be
explained, it will be challenging to take responsibility and
accountability. The framework used in this module is one example. It is not
a definitive framework but will provide a foundation for the rest of the
module. The focus is to apply the principles to guide considerations,
rather than to have a definite rule to apply them.
Other principles may be discussed in appropriate groups as needed. For
example, robustness, accuracy, and privacy are discussed within the safety
category. Although transparency and accountability are different
principles, this module treats them as a group, as they are closely
intertwined and interrelated.
The focus of this section is to introduce these categories of the ethical
framework and give some indications and examples of how they apply to
predictive models and the data they use. We will return to the principles
in this framework throughout the rest of the modules, showing how they
apply to the various aspects of the data and modeling processes.
Ethical Principles and Framework


### 1.1.12 Fairness

Fairness refers to impartial and just treatment without favoritism or
discrimination. Fairness often includes social context considerations
ensuring individuals are treated consistently. Fairness is a principle
where the aim is to protect human rights, such as dignity and equality, and
the right not to be discriminated against.
As fairness is defined under social context, there is no universal
criterion or objective standard that applies. Determining fairness of a
classification factor may depend on the domain, cultural, regulatory, and
legal contexts as well as an individual’s viewpoint.

Discrimination against certain groups, attributes, or categories, is
illegal and regulated in many countries. Such groups are often referred to
as protected groups or protected classes; examples of anti-discrimination
laws and regulations are discussed later in this section.
A proxy variable is a variable that is not a protected or sensitive
variable, but is strongly correlated with a protected characteristic. As
discussed earlier, Amazon built an algorithm using a predictive model to
automate the hiring process. The hiring algorithm was considered biased
against females even though the data did not include information on the sex
of candidates. Removing sensitive fields from the data didn’t help, as the
algorithm found proxies for sex. For example, the algorithm recognized when
the applicant was on a women’s sports-team or graduated from a women’s
college.

### 1.1.15 Fairness: Implementation and Governance

Fairness can also be considered in the context of implementation and
governance, which intends to ensure the overall process and model
implementation, monitoring, and maintenance is fair and not biased or
discriminatory. Even if fairness is considered in data collection, data
selection, and the modeling stage, biased results may occur during the
overall process and the implementation stage.
Efforts to achieve fairness in implementation will be more successful if
practitioners consider implementation aspects at the beginning of the
project. Even though a feature may seem innocuous when building the model,
in practice, it can end up responsible for a model unfairly discriminating
based on the implementation.
For an insurance example, even if the pricing or underwriting predictive
models may have considered the data and model fairness ensuring that only
actuarially sound factors were used, the marketing and commission strategy
may promote or demote certain customer groups, leading to potential
discrimination.
Also, it is important to be aware of instances in which a model may have
been carefully developed for one situation, but ultimately used for a
different one; this can lead to bias and unintended consequences.
Fairness: Implementation and Governance


### 1.1.16 Safety

Safety refers to the operational and technical sustainability aspects of
the data, model, and implementation. Safety can help protect aspects of
human rights and public safety, such as the right not to be harmed, the
right to privacy, freedom, the right to choose and autonomy, and the right
for well-being and care. Safety may help to protect and prevent harm from
misuse, poor design, unintended results, denial of autonomy, denial of
rights, or breach of privacy.
Accuracy and reliability are key components of safety considerations.
Accuracy means that the data has no errors and the models produce correct
results. Reliability means that a model, system, or prediction behaves
consistent with intentions and expectations. It is not necessarily true
that a model must be reliable if it is accurate. It is possible a highly
complex model may be accurate but may be unstable and unreliable in
interpretation.
Robustness is a quality where accuracy and reliability are maintained under
stressed, extreme, or unusual conditions.
Security is protecting a model or a system against possible attacks that
would breach integrity or privacy.
Safety concerns arise during data collection, data selection, model
building, and model implementation and governance processes.
Safety


### 1.1.17 Safety: Data

Data accuracy is a component of safety and is a desired trait in the data
extraction and collection process. A typical predictive modeling project
requires extensive data wrangling. Data reconciliation and validation are
done to ensure accuracy.
Data privacy is also an aspect of data safety. Organizations may obtain
customers’ personal information that should be kept safe. These
organizations will need to consider how personal information is stored and
used. Data may need to be encrypted and de-identified if required for
specific purposes. Even if data is de-identified, the possibility of re-
identification may cause harm or safety issues.
For example, in 2006, Netflix held a contest that challenged researchers to
find superior movie recommendation algorithms. Researchers were provided
with de-identified datasets and a prize was announced. Two researchers from
the University of Texas were able to re-identify a group of Netflix users
very quickly by comparing rating information against public non-anonymous
movie reviews on IMDb. A research paper was published describing how easy
it was to re-identify an individual and, due to security concerns, Netflix
had to cancel its prize and settled a lawsuit related to violation of the
Video Privacy Protection Act. (Narayanan & Shmatikov, 2008).
Some data collected may be behavior-induced, such as variables related to
lifestyle or wellness. Depending on how the data is collected, the behavior-
induced data may affect accuracy, reliability, consistency, or integrity.
An example is step-count data from a pedometer, that may have been affected
by dishonest behavior (affecting the integrity of the data) or the device's
inaccuracy (affecting the accuracy or reliability of the data).
Safety: Data


### 1.1.18 Safety: Analytics and Model

Analytical safety relates to analyzing the data in the model consistently
as intended. Modeling requires an appropriate understanding of the problem
definition, data, and algorithm. For example, causation and association
should be distinguished. One would need to avoid misusing or
misinterpreting some statistical features, such as Simpson’s paradox and P-
hacking.
Predictive models have an objective to maximize the accuracy of the
predictive power given the data set. This accuracy is also an aspect of
safety, as the predictive model aims to minimize any potential prediction
error. While accuracy is a desired trait from a technical standpoint, the
cost of false predictions can raise ethical questions. The model may be
chosen to minimize the overall cost of the false predictions, given the
costs of false positives (predicting as positive although that is not true)
and false negatives (predicting as negative although that is not true).
Models are not perfect, and there may be trade-offs between false positives
and false negatives.
A fire alarm is an example where the cost of false negatives (the alarm not
going off if there actually is a fire) outweighs the cost of false
positives (the annoyance of an alarm without a fire). This cost
consideration results in a reasonably sensitive alarm that might warn of
benign smoke coming from a cooking oven.
Safety: Analytics and Model

### 1.1.20 Transparency and Accountability: Data

Transparency, accountability, and auditability apply to data wrangling.
Data wrangling, which includes data collection, merge, mapping,
transformation, and conversion procedures, should be well-documented, and
the team should be accountable for any issues with the data. The data
process needs to be auditable to ensure it has clear ownership, is sound,
and reproducible.
Defining who owns the data relates to accountability. For example, data
ownership might not be clear if the data is de-identified or transformed
into metadata. Does the metadata belong to an individual or an organization
that collected and processed the individual inputs?
If one takes a photo of people at a public beach, who owns the picture?
What, if any, kind of consent was necessary before or after taking the
photo? If one publishes the unedited picture on a public website, what can
a person in the photo claim? What right do the people in the photo have?
Can the people in the picture claim ownership of the photo? Can the people
in the picture claim to be de-identified? While local regulations may
answer some of these inquiries, part of accountability relates to
clarifying data rights and ownership and ensuring the data is handled in an
ethical way. Accountability also means defining and clarifying the
responsibilities of the data collector and the data processor in relation
to such rights and ownership.
Transparency and Accountability: Data


### 1.1.21 Transparency and Accountability: Analytics and Model

Explainability is the extent to which the workings in a machine learning
algorithm can be explained in human terms. It means being transparent about
what variables are used, the extent to which they drive the response, and
how changing the inputs would impact the output. (U.K. Government Digital
Service, 2020).
Model explainability also refers to the ability to explain the model
design, algorithm, assumptions, and inferences. Inferences include
explaining a relationship between predictor variables and the predictions.
Model explainability refers to an interpretation of the model and
explanations for how the model is working as intended.
Black box models may be referred to as such since the algorithms are too
complex to be easily comprehended, people do not understand why or how the
model works, or the drivers behind the results cannot easily be explained.
From a regulatory perspective, black box models can refer to those where
the model mechanics are considered proprietary, and the organization is not
willing to submit them to regulatory scrutiny. Models built using more
advanced machine learning methods may be very difficult to interpret due to
the model structure. However, even simple models can sometimes be difficult
to understand. While you may be able to trace through the algorithm,
understanding the results and gaining insight into the predictors can still
be a challenge.
Transparency and Accountability: Analytics and Model


### 1.1.22 Transparency and Accountability: Implementation

Transparency and accountability issues also arise in implementation. Data
collection, data analysis, model calibration, and model predictions are all
performed by machines with the guidance of humans. Predictive modeling
projects may automate the workflow of the processes (e.g., data extraction,
data validation, and audit functions). Transparency requires that the
process and outcomes are clearly documented and communicated, such that it
can be repeated and audited. The analytics process includes the end-to-end
process, e.g., data collection and selection, data processing, model
selection and validation, model deployment and monitoring. Transparency as
it relates to the outcome means the ability to explain and justify the
effect of the model in relation to the business context, ethical values,
and other ethical principles (e.g., fairness).
Accountability requires that the process as well as the results are owned
by humans, not machines. Using machines to perform calculations and
orchestrate the workflow does not mean humans are exempt from
responsibility. As such, accountability includes having sufficient controls
in the automation. Human control may mean review, monitoring, and any other
interventions (e.g., a kill switch that allows an operator to immediately
shut down a machine even though it may damage it.).
What should be done when issues arise in the predictive models? Both
transparency and accountability are key considerations to address.
Transparency of the model would help understand the source of the issues;
transparency of the process would help communicate the issue with necessary
stakeholders, and accountability allows responsible agents to take
necessary actions.
Transparency and Accountability: Implementation

### 1.2.2 Introduction

In addition to ethical considerations, the work of actuaries is influenced
by the regulatory framework in which they work. Laws and regulations exist
in part to help protect the rights of individuals, to help assure fair
treatment for all people, and to prevent unfair discrimination. There are
several laws and regulations that are related to the collection and use of
data; it is very important that an actuary is aware of which laws and
regulations apply in a given situation.
Actuarial Standards of Practice (ASOPs) are promulgated by the Actuarial
Standards Board in the U.S. and help actuaries understand the professional
standards applicable to the performance of various actuarial tasks. Other
jurisdictions have similar standards. ASOPs covering risk classification,
data quality, actuarial communications, and modeling are especially
important for the actuary to be aware of in the context of building and
implementing predictive models.

### 1.2.4 Regulations

In practice, regulations are strict and must be followed. The law is the
law, so aside from interpreting legal language, whether or not an action is
compliant isn’t up for debate.
In the workplace, such compliance involves following the laws and rules
that apply to your organization. An organization and its employees must
comply with applicable laws and regulations; otherwise there will be
consequences such as sanctions, fines, financial losses, or reputational
risks. Additionally, organizations often have written policies, processes,
and tools that their employees are trained on and expected to follow. These
policies, often called a code of conduct, can be stricter than applicable
laws and regulations.
While ethics and regulations both aim for individuals and groups to act
morally and responsibly, they are different, yet complementary. In contrast
to ethics, laws and regulations are set boundaries that must be complied
with and are less open to personal interpretation.
Regulations


### 1.2.5 Intersection of Regulations and Ethics

Regulations and ethics are ultimately complementary standards that
typically work hand in hand. While the most ethical choice is not always
clear cut and has trade-offs and conflicts, it is an integral part of
compliance with regulations.
Sometimes it may feel that ethics hold individuals to a higher standard
than regulations, but that may not always be the case. While regulations
can be viewed as “just the rules” or the minimum that must be followed,
there is generally significant time, effort, and thought that went into the
creation of those rules. Ethics can be tied to both personal and
professional morals and values, which are generally quite broad and
overarching. However, there are some situations where laws and regulations
can demand more than what is ethically required.
Intersection of Regulations and Ethics


### 1.2.6 Regulations on Data Protection and Privacy

Just because you have access to data doesn’t necessarily mean you should or
are legally allowed to use it for any purpose. There are regulations and
professional standards surrounding the collection and use of data.
Most regulations are in place to protect consumers. As such, they typically
apply only, or at least more forcefully, to “personal data,” i.e., data
that can be used to identify an individual. When dealing with personal data
(processing, storing, analyzing, or even transferring), there will almost
certainly be laws that apply to your activities, and due care should be
taken to ensure you are complying with them.
However, these laws do not usually apply to data that has been anonymized
or de-identified to the point at which the data no longer qualifies as
personal data and cannot be re-identified. While you might need to
anonymize data before you can use it to comply with various laws, the act
of anonymization itself is classified as the processing of personal data
and needs to comply with the laws governing the use of the data. To comply
with these laws, it is important to understand the applicable standards and
methods in relation to de-identifying the data.
Regulations on Data Protection and Privacy

### 1.2.10 European Union

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) is a data privacy and
security law relating to collecting or using data of people in the EU. GDPR
is considered the toughest privacy and security law in the world. (Wolford,
n.d.). The following provides additional information.
The General Data Protection Regulation is a European Union law that was
implemented May 25, 2018 and requires organizations to safeguard personal
data and uphold the privacy rights of anyone in EU territory. The
regulation includes seven principles of data protection that must be
implemented and eight privacy rights that must be facilitated. It also
empowers member state-level data protection authorities to enforce the GDPR
with sanctions and fines. (GDPR.eu, 2020)
Any organization that processes the personal data of people in the EU must
comply with the GDPR. ‘Processing’ is a broad term that covers just about
anything you can do with data: collection, storage, transmission, analysis,
etc. ‘Personal data’ is any information that relates to a person, such as
names, email addresses, I.P. addresses, eye color, political affiliation,
and so on. (Proton Technologies AG, n.d.)
Personal data is any information that relates to an identified or
identifiable living individual. Different pieces of information, which
collected together can lead to the identification of a particular person,
also constitute personal data. Personal data that has been de-identified,
encrypted or pseudonymized but can be used to re-identify a person remains
personal data and falls within the scope of the GDPR. (European Commission,
n.d.)

The following list shows some of the laws and regulations to prevent

Redlining was an explicit mortgage lending and investment practice that

other financial services.

redlining. While geographic location or zip code is not a directly
protected class, there can be a high correlation between zip code and race,
ethnicity, national origin, and religion. As such, while credit
applications contain the full zip code, loan issuers can use only the first
three digits of the zip code in assessing credit-worthiness to prevent
redlining.

### 1.2.15 Risk Classification in Insurance

Unlike other industries, insurance is in the business of classifying
applicants to the extent the criteria relate to the insurance risk. In some
situations, grouping insureds into homogenous classes is considered to be
fair when it is based on differences in loss potential and not in a way
that discriminates against protected classes. Risk classification attempts
to categorize insurance applicants into multiple classes based on risk
profiles. The underwriting and risk classification process assigns a risk
classification to applicants and declines applications when certain
criteria are not met. Without underwriting and risk classification, riskier
applicants have an incentive to join the pool, whereas less risky
applicants have incentive to not join the pool, leading to adverse
selection. Risk classification is a necessary component for the
sustainability and soundness of an insurance system.
Actuarial fairness indicates that the level of premium can differ depending
on the level of risk of an individual. Risk classification is a core
concept in actuarial practice. Risk classification should be based on data
that are reasonable and identify actual drivers of loss. However, it is not
true that all predictive variables must be used in risk classification.
Many successful products are designed in a manner that rates do not
distinguish between certain classes or based on certain characteristics.
Problems are more likely to arise if there are unequal or unfair
requirements among competitors, which can lead to adverse selection. That
is, all competitors should be permitted to use the same variables, where
they see fit. Further, the disallowance of protected class variables in
risk classification helps to prevent a disproportionate impact on these
protected classes that does not reflect an actual greater potential for
loss.

### 1.2.16 Discrimination in Insurance

While insurance is in the discrimination business, not all discrimination
is fair. Certain limits and constraints exist, for example, using factors
such as race, national origin,* and religion. There may be variations in
determining factors (e.g., sex) as protected and prohibited for insurance
applications. Sex may be relevant to some insurance risks. However, some
regions restrict using sex at all for pricing. The EU prohibited the use of
sex as a pricing variable effective as of December 21, 2012 (European
Commission, 2012).
In the U.S., insurance companies are regulated at the state level and
regulation varies between states. Use of age, sex, and marital status may
or may not be allowed in insurance pricing depending on the type of
insurance and where the filing is being done. For example, New York State
prohibits issuers from discriminating against individuals based on sexual
orientation, gender identity or expression, or transgender status.

### 1.2.20 Ethical Principles and Framework

This module reviewed three groups of principles: fairness, safety, and
transparency and accountability. These are used to guide discussions in

Ethical Principles and Framework
Insurance Regulations
Unlike many other industries, insurance is in the business of risk
categorization to the extent it relates to the insurance risk. This is when
discrimination is considered fair and risk classification occurs. However,
certain limits and constraints exist, for example, using classification
factors such as race, national origin, and religion. There may be
variations in determining factors (e.g., sex) as protected and prohibited
for insurance applications, depending on domicile or type of products.
Comparison Between Ethical and Legal Issues
Ethics refers to standards of behavior defining how individuals, a
community, or society should act morally and responsibly in various
situations they may find themselves in. Regulations are strict and must be
followed. Regulations set a boundary for what one can or cannot do. There
may be cases where certain actions are both ethical and legal. However,
legality does not guarantee ethicality or vice versa.
Key Relevant Regulations Related to Data Protection, Privacy, And Anti-
Discrimination
This module noted that regulations exist to regulate privacy, data
collection and use, and discrimination. Discrimination against certain
groups, attributes, or categories is illegal and regulated in many
countries.

directly to the aforementioned ethical principles and framework. ASOPs 41
(Actuarial Communications), 23 (Data Quality), 56 (Modeling), and 12(Risk
